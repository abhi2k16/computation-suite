**Authors:** Luca Saverio, Michele Alessandro Bucci, Gianmarco Farro, Cédric Content, Denis Sipp
**Venue:** Data-Centric Engineering (Cambridge University Press), 2026, e37 — doi:10.1017/dce.2026.10066
**Affiliations:** Safran Tech, ONERA/DAAA, ONERA/MONHADE (INRIA-ONERA)

## 1. What the paper does

The paper proposes a general recipe for coupling an existing, mature PDE solver — here the compressible RANS solver **BROADCAST** (Fortran, differentiated with **Tapenade**) — with **PyTorch**, so that trainable corrections (scalar parameters, spatial fields, or neural networks) can be optimized end-to-end by gradient descent, while the underlying numerical solver (Newton/fixed-point iteration, sparse LU) is left untouched. The governing idea is deceptively simple:

$$R(w) + f_\vartheta(w) = 0$$

— the baseline discretized PDE residual $R(w)$ plus an additive, parametrized correction $f_\vartheta(w)$ — and the paper is essentially a careful, well-engineered answer to "how do you make $\vartheta$ trainable via `loss.backward()` without throwing away the solver's own Newton/LU machinery?"

Two solution strategies are formalized and demonstrated:

- **Explicit / residual-minimization** (§2.1.1, §2.2.1): when the *full state* $w_m$ is known (e.g. from a high-fidelity simulation), minimize $\|R(w_m)+f_\vartheta(w_m)\|_Q^2$ directly — no solver iteration needed, gradients flow through a single forward evaluation and its VJP.
- **Implicit layer** (§2.1.2, §2.2.2): when only *partial* state information is available (sparse velocity data, no density/temperature/turbulence-variable data), the PDE must actually be solved (`Rndlfxed-point`/Newton) to get $w$, and gradients w.r.t. $\vartheta$ are obtained via the **adjoint equation** $(\partial_w R + \partial_w f_\vartheta)^\top \lambda = \partial_w J^\top$, implemented as a custom `torch.autograd.Function` that overrides `backward()` — explicitly drawing the connection to **Deep Equilibrium Models** (Bai et al. 2019).

This machinery is then applied to two RANS closure problems built on the Spalart–Allmaras (SA) model: (i) a scalar production-term multiplier $\beta$ optimized against LES data on the 2D NASA Wall-Mounted Hump, and (ii) the eddy-viscosity field $\mu_t$, first reconstructed directly (§4.3) and then modeled with a small locality-constrained CNN trained across a 1500-case, 840-converged-case dataset of VKI LS-59 turbine-cascade geometries (§4.4) — a dataset the authors release openly on Zenodo.

## 2. Strengths

**a. The methodology is genuinely more careful than most "differentiable-CFD" papers.** Sections 2.1–2.2 do the unglamorous but essential work that many ML-for-CFD papers skip: correctly deriving gradients in a *weighted* inner-product space (the $Q$ matrix for the state residual, $M$ for the parameter space) and then explicitly handling the **change of variables** $\tilde\vartheta = N\vartheta$ (Cholesky factor of $M$) needed to hand a Euclidean-space gradient to a standard PyTorch optimizer (Appendix C). This is a real, easy-to-get-wrong detail — using the wrong metric for the gradient silently produces "working" but mathematically inconsistent optimization steps — and the paper is explicit about it rather than glossing over it.

**b. Legacy-code-friendly engineering.** Rather than reimplementing a CFD solver in a native ML framework (the more common approach, e.g. JAX-CFD-style differentiable solvers), the authors wrap **existing Fortran routines**, differentiated once via Tapenade, as `torch.autograd.Function` objects (`forward`/`backward`/optionally `jvp`), composed into a `torch.nn.Module`. This is exactly the kind of pattern that lets a 10–20-year-old industrial CFD code become trainable *without rewriting its numerics* — a genuinely practical contribution, and one the paper is honest about being the actual novelty ("the originality... lies not in the use of differentiable programming itself, but in the construction of a practical end-to-end framework," §1). The comparison to **FTorch** (Atkinson et al. 2025) is apt and appropriately cited.

**c. Architectural discipline in the CNN.** The eddy-viscosity CNN (Appendix D) is deliberately built with kernel widths **no larger than the numerical scheme's own stencil**, specifically so the correction term's Jacobian sparsity pattern matches $\partial_w R$ exactly — preserving the solver's sparse-LU machinery instead of forcing a fallback to matrix-free methods. This is a nice example of letting the numerics dictate the ML architecture rather than the reverse, and it's explained clearly with a helpful figure (Fig. D1).

**d. Honest reporting of a partial/mixed result.** The paper does not oversell the CNN closure experiment. It explicitly states that a strongly local (no wall-distance, stencil-width-limited) algebraic model cannot fully capture separated/wake regions, and the results confirm it: errors are small in attached boundary layers but "remain nonnegligible on the suction side... near the trailing edge, where flow separation occurs" (§4.4.2). This kind of transparent negative-adjacent result is good scientific practice and rarer than it should be in this literature.

**e. Reproducibility.** The VKI LS-59 dataset is released on Zenodo (DOI given), BROADCAST is open-source on GitHub, and the LES reference data are publicly available from NASA. The paper earned Cambridge's Open Data badge. The dataset generation is also validated with real statistical rigor in Appendix B — PCA-based dimensionality reduction (4 geometric modes capture 99.9% variance; 50 modes needed for the conservative state), a Gaussian-process surrogate, and Sobol sensitivity indices — which is more design-of-experiments diligence than most CFD-ML papers bother with.

**f. Simple but appropriate uncertainty quantification.** The 10-run training ensemble (§4.4.2, Fig. 16) is a cheap, standard, but well-executed epistemic-uncertainty proxy, and the authors show the ensemble standard deviation spatially correlates with regions of higher prediction error — a useful sanity check that the ensemble spread is actually informative rather than decorative.

## 3. Weaknesses, gaps, and points worth pressing on

**a. The two flagship results are not scientifically equivalent, and the paper somewhat blurs this.** The $\beta$-correction experiment (§4.1) is a genuine data-assimilation exercise against **independent LES ground truth** — a real test of whether the framework can pull a RANS solution toward higher-fidelity data. The $\mu_t$/CNN experiment (§4.3–4.4), by contrast, trains the network to reproduce **the SA model's own eddy-viscosity output** on a dataset generated *by the SA model itself*. This is a legitimate and useful engineering test (can the pipeline learn a nonlocal-in-principle field under strict locality constraints, does the training/optimization machinery scale to a dataset of hundreds of cases, does the sparsity-preserving architecture actually preserve the intended sparsity), but it is **not** a demonstration that the ML correction *improves* turbulence modeling — at best it shows the CNN can *approximate* SA within its own locality budget, with the largest errors precisely where SA itself is known to be weakest (separation, wake). The paper does flag this honestly in prose ("building an algebraic turbulence model capable of reproducing... SA is an extremely challenging task," §4), but a reader skimming the abstract could easily walk away thinking the CNN closure was validated against high-fidelity data the way the $\beta$ case was. I'd want the abstract/conclusion to state more sharply that the CNN result is a **pipeline/architecture validation**, not yet a **model-improvement** result — that combination (CNN + LES/DNS ground truth) is explicitly deferred to future work.

**b. No 3D case, and no case beyond a few hundred thousand cells.** Both benchmarks are 2D (a hump cross-section, a linear blade cascade at 300×120 and 408×108 cells). For a paper motivated by "industrial CFD codes... on large clusters" and "high-performance applications such as turbines in jet engines" (§1), the absence of any 3D demonstration — where sparse-LU adjoint solves and mesh sizes become dramatically more expensive — is a real gap. The implicit strategy already costs "approximately one minute" per outer iteration on a ~44k-cell 2D mesh; it's unclear (and not discussed) how this scales to a realistic 3D industrial mesh (tens of millions of cells), where direct sparse-LU factorization of the adjoint system is often no longer tractable and iterative/Krylov methods (mentioned only in passing, "GMRes") would be required with nontrivial preconditioning.

**c. Structured-mesh dependency is real, not just an implementation detail.** The paper repeatedly asserts that the method "could also be implemented for unstructured [meshes]" (§3.4) provided one supplies the JVP and Jacobian sparsity pattern — but this is not demonstrated, and it understates the difficulty: unstructured connectivity breaks the clean $(i,j)$-indexing that makes the CNN's stencil-locality trick (and the associated sparsity-preserving Jacobian argument) straightforward. A GNN-based analogue would likely be needed (the paper does cite Quattromini et al.'s GNN-based data-assimilation work as a "complementary line of research," but doesn't attempt to unify the two approaches). This is fine as future work, but the framing sometimes reads as though the extension were a mostly mechanical engineering task rather than a substantive one.

**d. Optimizer choices are inconsistent across experiments without justification.** L-BFGS is used for the implicit $\beta$ case (50 iterations) and for the CNN closure (mini-batch, 100), while SGD is used for the explicit $\beta$ case (2000 iterations) and the explicit $\mu_t$ reconstruction (2000 iterations). The choice is plausible on cost grounds (L-BFGS's curvature information is worth it when each iteration is expensive — i.e., implicit solves — while SGD suits cheap repeated explicit evaluations), but this reasoning is never stated, and no ablation compares optimizers on the same problem. A reader can't tell whether the reported convergence behavior (e.g., the implicit $\beta$ case's relatively modest ~5× loss reduction over 50 L-BFGS iterations vs. the explicit case's "several orders of magnitude" reduction over 2000 SGD iterations, Fig. 4 vs. Fig. 9) reflects the optimizer, the loss landscape, or the fundamentally different objectives being minimized (a *physical* velocity-mismatch loss vs. a *residual-norm* loss) — these are genuinely different quantities and the comparison as currently presented invites an apples-to-oranges reading.

**e. $\beta$ is acknowledged, but perhaps under-examined, as an inadequate control variable.** The paper cites Franceschini et al. (2020) to explain why the scalar production-multiplier $\beta$ "is not sufficiently flexible to accurately reproduce the high-fidelity data in the recirculation region" (§4.1.2) — a useful, honest citation — but doesn't push further into *why* the framework itself couldn't discover a better-parametrized correction here (e.g., a spatially local NN-based $\beta_\vartheta(w)$, which the abstract's own general formulation (2.12) supports). Given the framework's stated generality, testing a richer $\beta$ parametrization on the *same* LES-validated Hump case (rather than only on the SA-only VKI dataset) would have been a more direct test of whether ML richness actually buys accuracy against real high-fidelity data — currently that combination is never tried.

**f. Turbulent Prandtl number and other simplifications are fixed, not questioned.** $\text{Pr}_t = 0.9$ is used throughout and defended only as "an acceptable baseline choice for... adiabatic configurations" (§3.1) — reasonable for the demonstrated cases, but worth flagging since it's one of several places where a "known-imperfect but standard" RANS assumption is retained unmodified alongside the paper's data-driven correction of a *different* term; there's no discussion of whether errors misattributed to the eddy-viscosity/production terms could partly stem from this fixed assumption.

**g. Minor clarity issues.**
   - The paper states the implicit $\beta$ optimization's loss "starts at $3.245\times10^{-4}$... reached value is $6.611\times10^{-5}$" (Fig. 4 caption) — worth double-checking against the figure itself since the surrounding text elsewhere uses different exponent conventions (e.g. $10^{-6}$ tolerance thresholds are stated as plain "$10^{6}$" in a couple of places due to what looks like a LaTeX-to-text formatting slip in the OCR/typesetting — likely an artifact of the PDF's math rendering rather than the authors' actual manuscript, but worth being aware of if quoting numbers directly from the extracted text).
   - The paper's own Table 1 (DOE parameter ranges) lists identical min/max values for four of the six parameters ("Distance of the 2° point...," "Δx...," "Δy...," each at ±0.15/±0.2), which reads as though those parameters were held at fixed magnitude bounds rather than varied over a genuine range — this is very likely a formatting/rendering issue in the extracted table (symmetric ± bounds collapsing to one printed number) rather than an actual experimental-design flaw, but the table as rendered doesn't fully explain itself.
   - The 3D turbomachinery motivation (jet engine turbines, §1) sits somewhat awkwardly next to results that are exclusively 2D — not a flaw in the work performed, but a mismatch between framing and demonstrated scope worth resolving in future revisions.

## 4. Positioning relative to prior work

The related-work framing is solid and mostly fair:
- It correctly distinguishes itself from **PINNs** (Raissi et al. 2019) — here the discretized state is still produced by the *existing numerical solver*, not represented by a neural network ansatz — while honestly noting that the explicit residual-minimization variant (§2.1.1) is "conceptually related to PINNs" in its use of residual-norm minimization.
- It correctly distinguishes itself from most incompressible ML-RANS work (Ströfer and Xiao 2021; Zhang et al. 2023), which exploits the Pope (1975) exact Reynolds-stress decomposition and continuous-adjoint machinery unavailable in the compressible setting — a real and underappreciated reason compressible ML-RANS work is rarer.
- The comparison to **GNN-based data assimilation** (Quattromini et al. 2025a, 2025b) is appropriately framed as complementary rather than competing, though as noted above (§3.c), a more direct discussion of when one would choose structured-CNN-on-structured-mesh vs. GNN-on-unstructured-mesh would strengthen the paper.
- The **Deep Equilibrium Models** connection (Bai et al. 2019) is the right theoretical frame for the implicit layer and is used correctly, not just name-dropped — Appendix A's Listing 3 makes the correspondence concrete (the outer Newton solve plays the role of the DEQ fixed-point solve; the adjoint linear solve plays the role of DEQ's implicit-function-theorem backward pass).

---

## 5. Overall assessment

This is a solid, well-engineered systems/methodology paper rather than a turbulence-modeling breakthrough, and it's most useful to read as such. Its real contribution is a carefully-derived, correctly-scaled (in the right inner-product spaces), and honestly-validated **integration pattern** for making legacy Fortran/AD-based CFD solvers first-class differentiable citizens inside PyTorch — something that matters a great deal for industrial adoption of ML-augmented CFD, precisely because it *doesn't* require throwing away decades of numerical-methods investment. The math in §2 is careful and, as far as I can check without re-deriving every line, internally consistent (the KKT/adjoint derivation, the Cholesky change-of-variables for the parameter metric, and the sparsity-preservation argument for the CNN's Jacobian coupling with $\partial_w R$ all check out logically).

The physics results are more mixed than the framing initially suggests: the LES-validated $\beta$-correction case is a genuine (if modest, given $\beta$'s known rigidity) demonstration of the pipeline's value against real high-fidelity data, while the CNN eddy-viscosity closure is best read as a proof-of-concept for the *architecture and training pipeline* rather than a turbulence-model-improvement result, since its target is the SA model's own output rather than independent high-fidelity data. The paper is transparent about this limitation in its prose, which I'd credit, but a more careful reader needs to actively parse the difference — it would strengthen the paper to state this distinction explicitly in the abstract and conclusions rather than leaving it implicit.

The most valuable near-term follow-ups the authors themselves identify (extending the implicit/DEQ strategy to the full turbine dataset, training a $\beta$-style NN correction against real LES data with the CNN's locality machinery, going to richer architectures or wall-distance-aware features) are exactly the right next steps, and the released dataset plus open-source solver make those follow-ups genuinely reproducible by other groups — which is, on balance, the paper's most durable contribution.
